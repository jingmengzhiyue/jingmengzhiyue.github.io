Chapter 7: Cross-Modal Diffusion — Video, 3D, Audio, and Discrete Data

Diffusion models first broke through in image generation, but their core idea — starting from a simple distribution and approaching a complex data distribution through iterative denoising — is not limited to two-dimensional pixels. In recent years, researchers have extended the diffusion framework to a range of modalities, including video, three-dimensional geometry, audio, and discrete symbol sequences, creating the active frontier of cross-modal diffusion. This chapter walks through these directions one by one, focusing on three questions: what is hard about each modality, which part of the diffusion framework the main methods change, and the commonalities and trade-offs among them.

Note

This chapter keeps the notation from earlier chapters: $t$ is the diffusion time step, $T$ is the total number of diffusion steps, and $\epsilon_\theta$ is the noise prediction network. To avoid confusion, the video frame count is written $N_f$, not $T$.

7.1 Video Diffusion Models

Video adds a time dimension on top of images, and the core challenge in generation is temporal coherence: objects should change continuously in shape, color, and position from one frame to the next, rather than flickering and deforming the way independent per-frame sampling does. Video diffusion models must model temporal dynamics effectively while preserving spatial detail.

7.1.1 A Framework for Temporal Modeling in Video Generation

A video can be represented as a tensor $v \in \mathbb{R}^{N_f \times C \times H \times W}$. Video diffusion models usually perform “temporal inflation” on top of a pretrained image model — that is, they insert temporal layers responsible for cross-frame interaction into the U-Net or Transformer. 7.1.2 Video LDM: Video Generation with Aligned Latents

Video LDM (Blattmann et al., CVPR 2023)1 is the pioneering work that extended latent diffusion models (LDM, §3.4) from images to video. The core idea is to reuse the autoencoder and denoising network of a pretrained image LDM and learn only “temporal alignment” — inserting temporal layers into the U-Net (temporal attention and 3D convolution, learnably mixed with the outputs of the original spatial layers), while also adding temporal layers to the VAE decoder to keep frames coherent.

Video LDM’s forward noising process matches that of the image LDM, except that it acts on the sequence of latents obtained by encoding each frame, $z \in \mathbb{R}^{N_f \times C' \times H' \times W'}$:

$$ q(z_t \mid z_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\left(z_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, z_{t-1}, \beta_t \mathbf{I}\right) $$

where $z_0 = \mathcal{E}(v)$ is the output of the VAE encoder (applied frame by frame), and $\beta_t$ is the noise schedule parameter.

Notation:

SymbolMeaning
$z_t$Latent at diffusion time step $t$ (containing all frames)
$\mathcal{E}$VAE encoder
$\beta_t$Noise variance parameter at step $t$
$\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$Gaussian distribution with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$

Another key design in Video LDM is temporal fine-tuning of the decoder. The image VAE’s decoder has never seen video, so decoding frames independently causes flicker. Video LDM therefore inserts temporal layers into the decoder and fine-tunes it adversarially with a patch-discriminating video discriminator built from 3D convolutions, keeping the decoded result coherent across frames. The encoder stays frozen, which keeps the latent space compatible with the original image LDM (so the image model can be reused directly).

7.1.3 Stable Video Diffusion (SVD): Scaling Up Video Diffusion

Stable Video Diffusion (Blattmann et al., 2023)2 carries out a systematic study of training scale on top of Video LDM, proposing and validating a three-stage training paradigm:

  1. Text-to-image pretraining: built on Stable Diffusion 2.1, laying the foundation for visual quality and semantic understanding;
  2. Large-scale video pretraining: trained on a large video dataset that has been systematically curated (including automatic labeling and filtering), to learn motion priors;
  3. High-quality video fine-tuning: fine-tuned on a smaller but higher-quality video subset to improve generation fidelity.

One important conclusion of the paper is that carefully curated pretraining data is essential to final video quality. SVD follows the Video LDM setup, inserting temporal convolution and temporal attention after every spatial convolution and attention layer, adding roughly 656M parameters and bringing the U-Net to about 1.52B parameters in total2. The model controls motion magnitude through micro-conditioning such as motion_bucket_id, and controls frame rate through a frame-rate micro-condition.

The publicly released SVD weights are mainly image-to-video models: SVD generates 14 frames and SVD-XT generates 25, both at 576×1024 resolution. They are trained with the EDM framework (§2.4) and use classifier-free guidance (CFG).

7.1.4 AnimateDiff: A Plug-and-Play Motion Module

AnimateDiff (Guo et al., ICLR 2024)3 takes a lighter-weight approach: instead of modifying the weights of a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, it introduces a separate motion module and trains only that module on video data.

The motion module is essentially a set of Transformer layers that perform self-attention along the time axis (with temporal positional encoding and a zero-initialized output projection), inserted at every resolution level of the Stable Diffusion U-Net. During training, the image backbone is frozen and only the motion module is updated. The biggest advantage of this design is transferability: a trained motion module can be dropped directly into any Stable Diffusion model derived from the same base — including fine-tuned or personalized versions (such as DreamBooth or LoRA, §4.5) — to “animate” it, with no retraining per model. The paper also introduces MotionLoRA, which adapts the motion module to new motion patterns (such as different camera movements) with a small amount of data and compute.

AnimateDiff’s denoising network can be written as “frozen image parameters + trainable motion parameters”:

$$ \hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_{\theta_{\text{img}}, \theta_{\text{motion}}}\left( z_t, t, c_{\text{text}} \right), \qquad \theta_{\text{img}} \text{ frozen, only } \theta_{\text{motion}} \text{ trainable} $$

At sampling time, we denoise iteratively as usual to obtain $z_0$, then decode frame by frame to get the video $v_{\text{gen}} = \mathcal{D}(z_0)$. The training objective is still the standard noise prediction loss $\left\lVert \epsilon - \hat{\epsilon} \right\rVert_2^2$.

Notation:

SymbolMeaning
$v_{\text{gen}}$Generated video
$z_t$Latent at diffusion time step $t$ (all frames)
$c_{\text{text}}$Text condition embedding
$\theta_{\text{img}}$Frozen image U-Net parameters (replaceable with any personalized version)
$\theta_{\text{motion}}$Trainable parameters of the motion module
$\mathcal{D}$VAE decoder

7.1.5 Latte: A Latent Diffusion Transformer

Latte (Ma et al., 2024)4 replaces the U-Net backbone of video diffusion with a Transformer, validating the effectiveness of the DiT (§6.1) architecture for video generation. Latte first extracts spacetime tokens from video latents, then models the video distribution with a stack of Transformer blocks. To cope with the huge number of video tokens, the paper designs four efficient variants from the angle of “how to factorize the spatial and temporal dimensions”:

  • Variant 1 (interleaved fusion): spatial Transformer blocks (attention only within a single frame) and temporal Transformer blocks (attention only across frames at the same spatial position) stacked alternately;
  • Variant 2 (late fusion): the same number of blocks, but all spatial blocks stacked first, followed by all temporal blocks;
  • Variant 3 (factorized attention): inside each Transformer block, spatial self-attention first, then temporal self-attention;
  • Variant 4 (factorized attention heads): split the heads of multi-head attention into two groups, one for space and one for time, then merge them at the end.

The paper also systematically compares video patch embedding schemes (per-frame tiling vs. cross-frame spacetime tube tiling), ways of injecting time-step / class information, temporal positional encodings, and training strategies; variant 1 performs best in experiments. Latte achieved state-of-the-art results at the time on four standard datasets — FaceForensics, SkyTimelapse, UCF101, and Taichi-HD — and extends to text-to-video generation4.

7.1.6 Sora: A Unified Spacetime DiT Paradigm

Sora (OpenAI, 2024)5 is a landmark work in video generation. Its technical report only disclosed high-level methods, which can be summarized as follows:

  1. Video compression network: instead of encoding frame by frame, Sora trained an autoencoder that compresses in both time and space, mapping video to latents with lower resolution and a shorter time dimension, then training and generating in this latent space.

  2. Spacetime patches and a diffusion Transformer: the compressed latents are cut into spacetime patches to serve as Transformer tokens. Since an image is just a video with one frame, this representation can handle images and video uniformly. Sora trains directly on data at native resolution, duration, and aspect ratio, rather than cropping video to a fixed size; at generation time, it controls output size by arranging grids of noise patches of different sizes. The report states that Sora is a diffusion Transformer trained to predict the original “clean” patches from noisy ones.

  3. Re-captioning: following the approach of DALL·E 3, first train a highly descriptive video captioning model to generate detailed captions for all training videos; at inference time, use GPT to expand the user’s short prompt into a detailed description, significantly improving adherence to complex text instructions.

Because implementation details are not public, the following uses only the general diffusion reverse process to illustrate the denoising step for models like Sora:

$$ p_\theta(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, c) = \mathcal{N}\left(z_{t-1};\, \mu_\theta(z_t, t, c),\, \Sigma_\theta(t)\right) $$

where $z_t$ is the compressed spacetime latent, $c$ is the text condition, and $\mu_\theta$ is given by the Transformer network.

$z_t$Compressed spacetime latent

7.1.7 CogVideo, CogVideoX, and the Open-Source Ecosystem

CogVideo (Hong et al., 2022) is an early open-source large-scale text-to-video model, but it is not a diffusion model: built on the pretrained text-to-image model CogView2, it uses an autoregressive Transformer to generate video tokens and adopts a hierarchical generation strategy — first generating low-frame-rate keyframes, then generating intermediate frames through interpolation. Its successor CogVideoX (Yang et al., ICLR 2025)6 moves to the diffusion framework, introducing a 3D causal VAE (which compresses in both space and time and is causal along the time axis, so the first frame can be encoded independently as an image), a Transformer with “expert-adaptive LayerNorm,” and 3D full attention, improving generation quality and long-video capability. Recent open-source video diffusion models (such as HunyuanVideo and Wan) also commonly follow the technical route of “3D causal VAE + spacetime DiT + flow matching training.”

The core trade-off in temporal modeling is computation. Suppose each frame has $h \times w$ tokens in latent space, with $N_f$ frames in total:

  • 3D full attention attends over all $N_f h w$ tokens together, giving the strongest expressiveness, but at a cost of $O\left( (N_f h w)^2 \right)$;
  • Factorized spacetime attention (within frames first, then across frames, as in Latte variant 3) costs $O\left( N_f h w \cdot (h w + N_f) \right)$, which is much cheaper at high resolution and long video lengths, but information across frames and positions can only be exchanged indirectly.

7.1.8 Long Video and Autoregressive Video Diffusion

The models above generate a clip of fixed length in one pass. To generate longer videos, common approaches include:

  • Segment generation + overlap: condition the next segment on the last few frames of the previous one (a common usage for models such as SVD);
  • Diffusion Forcing7: during training, sample a noise level independently for each token (frame) in the sequence, so the model can both denoise the whole sequence like a diffusion model and “generate what comes next given what came before” like an autoregressive model, enabling variable-length, continuously extendable sequence generation. This idea of “different noise levels per frame” has become an important foundation for autoregressive video diffusion and world models.

7.2 Three-Dimensional Diffusion Models

Three-dimensional generation requires the model to output data with spatial structure (point clouds, meshes, voxels, implicit fields, 3D Gaussians, or multi-view images), and the core challenges are multi-view consistency and valid geometry / topology. At the same time, high-quality 3D training data is far scarcer than image data. Two broad paradigms have emerged: direct 3D diffusion (training a diffusion model on some 3D representation) and 3D generation with 2D priors (using a pretrained 2D diffusion model to guide the optimization or reconstruction of a 3D representation).

7.2.1 Direct Diffusion on 3D Representations

Point-E: Three-Stage Point Cloud Generation

Point-E (Nichol et al., OpenAI, 2022)8 breaks text-to-3D generation into a cascade of three steps:

  1. Text to image: use a GLIDE text-to-image diffusion model fine-tuned on 3D renders to generate a synthetic viewpoint image;
  2. Image to coarse point cloud: conditioned on that image, use a diffusion model to generate a coarse point cloud of 1024 points (with colors);
  3. Point cloud upsampling: use another diffusion model conditioned on the low-resolution point cloud to refine it to 4096 points.

If a mesh is needed, Point-E also trains a regression model to predict an SDF from the point cloud, then extracts the surface with Marching Cubes. Point-E generates quickly (a minute or two on a single GPU), but has limited fidelity because point clouds lack surface topology information.

Shap-E: Conditional Diffusion of Implicit Functions

Shap-E (Jun & Nichol, OpenAI, 2023)9 instead generates the parameters of an implicit function, which can be rendered either as a textured mesh or as a neural radiance field (NeRF). Training proceeds in two stages:

  1. Encoder training: train an encoder that maps a 3D asset deterministically to the parameters of an implicit function (an MLP);
  2. Conditional diffusion: train a conditional diffusion model on the encoder’s output parameters, generating new implicit-function parameters conditioned on text or an image.

The paper finds that although Shap-E must model a higher-dimensional output space and support multiple representations at once, it still converges faster than Point-E, with comparable or better sample quality, and directly yields a renderable textured mesh.

Voxel, Point Cloud, and Triplane Diffusion

Other representative representations include:

  • Point cloud diffusion: for example PVD (Point-Voxel Diffusion), which denoises point coordinates directly with a point-voxel hybrid network;
  • Implicit field / voxel diffusion: for example Diffusion-SDF, which diffuses over implicit or voxelized representations such as signed distance fields (SDFs);
  • Triplane diffusion (e.g., NFD): encode a 3D shape into 2D feature maps on three orthogonal planes, then diffuse over those feature maps — reusing 2D diffusion architectures while substantially lowering computational complexity.

7.2.2 Score Distillation Sampling (SDS): Generating 3D with 2D Priors

DreamFusion (Poole et al., Google, ICLR 2023 outstanding paper)10 proposed an entirely new 3D generation paradigm: without training any 3D diffusion model, it treats a pretrained 2D text-to-image diffusion model as a “critic” and uses Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) to optimize a differentiable 3D representation (such as a NeRF) so that images rendered from any viewpoint look “like” real images matching the text description.

Core SDS Equation

Given the parameters $\phi$ of a differentiable 3D representation (such as a NeRF’s MLP weights) and the image rendered from viewpoint $\pi$, $x = g(\phi, \pi)$, SDS updates $\phi$ iteratively with the following gradient:

$$ \nabla_\phi \mathcal{L}_{\text{SDS}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, \pi, \epsilon} \left[ w(t) \left( \epsilon_\theta(x_t; t, c) - \epsilon \right) \frac{\partial x}{\partial \phi} \right] $$

where $x_t = \alpha_t x + \sigma_t \epsilon$ is the forward-noised rendered image (continuous-time notation, as in §5.1.3), $\epsilon_\theta$ is the noise prediction of the frozen pretrained 2D diffusion model, $c$ is the text condition, and $w(t)$ is a time-step weight.

Where does this gradient come from? If we differentiate the diffusion model’s training loss $w(t) \lVert \epsilon_\theta(x_t; t, c) - \epsilon \rVert^2$ directly with respect to $\phi$, the chain rule produces a U-Net Jacobian term $\partial \epsilon_\theta / \partial x_t$, which is expensive and unstable to compute. DreamFusion found that simply dropping this term works even better, giving the concise form above. Since $\epsilon_\theta - \epsilon$ is proportional to the (negative) score difference, SDS can be understood as using the score provided by the 2D diffusion model to push each viewpoint’s rendering toward “high-probability image regions given the text.”

Notation:

SymbolMeaning
$\phi$Trainable parameters of the 3D representation (e.g., a NeRF)
$g(\phi, \pi)$Differentiable rendering function for images from viewpoint $\pi$
$x$Rendered 2D image
$x_t$Image after noising $x$
$\epsilon_\theta$Noise prediction network of the frozen pretrained 2D diffusion model
$c$Text condition / prompt
$w(t)$Time-step-dependent weight coefficient
$\alpha_t, \sigma_t$Signal and noise coefficients

Variants and Improvements

SDS suffers from oversaturation, over-smoothing, poor diversity, and the “polyhedron / Janus problem” (an object growing a frontal face when viewed from many directions), and requires a very large CFG scale (DreamFusion uses 100). Later improvements include:

  • Magic3D (Lin et al., NVIDIA, CVPR 2023): a coarse-to-fine two-stage approach — first optimize a coarse neural field with a low-resolution diffusion prior, then extract a DMTet mesh and fine-tune it with a high-resolution latent diffusion model;
  • Fantasia3D (Chen et al., ICCV 2023): decouples geometry and appearance (material) and optimizes them separately;
  • ProlificDreamer / VSD (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2023)11: introduces Variational Score Distillation, treating the 3D parameters as a distribution rather than a single point, and replacing the random noise $\epsilon$ in SDS with a LoRA diffusion model $\epsilon_\psi$ fine-tuned online on the current renderings:
$$ \nabla_\phi \mathcal{L}_{\text{VSD}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, \pi, \epsilon} \left[ w(t) \left( \epsilon_\theta(x_t; t, c) - \epsilon_\psi(x_t; t, c, \pi) \right) \frac{\partial x}{\partial \phi} \right] $$

VSD works at a normal CFG scale (such as 7.5), substantially reducing oversaturation and over-smoothing, with better detail and diversity. It shares the same “difference of two scores” idea as distribution matching distillation (DMD, §5.3.4) in Chapter 5;

  • MVDream (Shi et al., ICLR 2024): trains a multi-view diffusion model that can generate multiple consistent viewpoints at once, and uses it in place of the single-view 2D prior for SDS, substantially alleviating the Janus problem.

7.2.3 Multi-View-Consistent Diffusion and 3D Reconstruction

SV3D: From Video Diffusion to Multi-View Synthesis

SV3D (Voleti et al., Stability AI, ECCV 2024)12 cleverly repurposes a video diffusion model into a multi-view diffusion model: an “orbital video” shot by circling an object is itself a set of multi-view images, with each frame corresponding to one viewpoint. Building on Stable Video Diffusion, SV3D adds camera pose conditioning (elevation and azimuth), enabling camera-controllable multi-view synthesis from a single image.

Its 3D generation pipeline is also coarse-to-fine: first optimize a NeRF supervised by the generated multi-view images, then extract a DMTet mesh for fine-tuning. For regions invisible outside the generated viewpoints, SV3D introduces a Masked SDS loss that applies score distillation only to those invisible regions; it also jointly optimizes a decoupled lighting model to reduce the problem of lighting being “baked into” the texture.

Consistent123 and Free3D

  • Consistent123 (Weng et al., 2023)13: synthesizes multiple novel views at once, passing information between views through cross-view attention and shared self-attention, and uses progressive classifier-free guidance to improve multi-view consistency in one-image-to-3D;
  • Free3D (Zheng & Vedaldi, CVPR 2024)14: like Zero-1-to-3, it starts from a pretrained 2D image generator and fine-tunes it for novel-view synthesis, but uses no explicit 3D representation and trains no additional 3D reconstruction network. Its key is ray conditioning normalization (RCN), which tells each pixel its own viewing direction; combined with lightweight multi-view attention and shared multi-view noise, this improves consistency across views.

Combining 3D Gaussian Splatting with Diffusion

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become a new vehicle for 3D generation because it is differentiable and renders extremely fast:

  • DreamGaussian (Tang et al., ICLR 2024): combines SDS with 3DGS, replacing NeRF’s occupancy pruning with progressive densification of 3D Gaussians, which converges much faster; it then converts the Gaussians to a textured mesh and fine-tunes in UV space. Generating a high-quality textured mesh from a single image takes about 2 minutes, roughly 10 times faster than existing methods at the time;
  • Diffusing directly on 3D Gaussian parameters: treating each Gaussian’s position, covariance, color, and opacity as the object of diffusion is an active research direction;
  • Feed-forward reconstruction: for example Splatter Image (Szymanowicz et al., CVPR 2024) regresses 3D Gaussian parameters from a single image in one network pass (it is itself a regression model, not a diffusion model). A common efficient pipeline in practice is “multi-view diffusion model generates multi-view images + feed-forward large reconstruction model (LRM-style) reconstructs 3D,” completing one-image / text-to-3D in seconds to minutes.

7.3 Audio Diffusion Models

An audio signal is a one-dimensional waveform that varies over time, $a(t) \in \mathbb{R}$ (here $t$ is physical time), with strong temporal structure and frequency characteristics. Because raw waveforms have a high sampling rate (16–48 kHz) and extremely long sequences, audio diffusion models usually do not generate directly on the waveform; instead they diffuse in the time-frequency domain (such as mel spectrograms) or in a learned latent space, then recover the waveform with a vocoder or decoder.

7.3.1 The Spectrogram Latent Diffusion Paradigm

AudioLDM (Liu et al., ICML 2023)15 is a representative work applying the LDM framework to text-to-audio generation. Its pipeline is:

  1. Convert the audio waveform to a mel spectrogram $X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times F}$ via STFT;
  2. Compress it to a latent $z \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times L' \times F'}$ with a VAE trained on mel spectrograms;
  3. Train a diffusion model in the latent space. During training, condition on CLAP’s audio embedding $E^{x}$; during sampling, swap in CLAP’s text embedding $E^{y}$ — because CLAP aligns audio and text in the same embedding space, the model can perform text-to-audio generation without paired “text-audio” data;
  4. After generation, recover the mel spectrogram with the VAE decoder, then convert it to a waveform with the HiFi-GAN vocoder.

AudioLDM’s forward process matches that of the image LDM (using $n$ for the diffusion step, to avoid confusion with the spectrogram’s time axis):

$$ q(z_n \mid z_{n-1}) = \mathcal{N}\left(z_n; \sqrt{1 - \beta_n}\, z_{n-1}, \beta_n \mathbf{I}\right) $$

The training objective is noise prediction (conditioned on the audio embedding $E^{x}$ during training):

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{z_0, \epsilon, n} \left[ \left\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(z_n, n, E^{x}) \right\rVert_2^2 \right] $$

Notation:

SymbolMeaning
$X$Mel spectrogram ($L$ time frames, $F$ mel bands)
$z$Audio latent after VAE compression
$E^{x}$ / $E^{y}$CLAP audio embedding (training) / CLAP text embedding (sampling)
$\epsilon$Injected Gaussian noise
$n$Discrete diffusion time step
$\epsilon_\theta$Noise prediction network (U-Net)

Key AudioLDM configuration: AudioLDM-S has about 181 million parameters and AudioLDM-L about 739 million; the number of diffusion steps is $N = 1000$, with a linear noise schedule from $\beta_1 = 0.0015$ to $\beta_N = 0.0195$; sampling uses 200 DDIM steps and a CFG scale of $w = 2.0$ (in the CFG convention of the original AudioLDM paper)15.

The advantage of spectrogram / latent-space diffusion is this: mel spectrograms organize a high-sample-rate waveform into a regular, image-like two-dimensional representation (for 16 kHz audio with a hop length of 160 and 64 mel bands, that’s $100 \times 64 = 6400$ values per second, about $1/2.5$ of the raw waveform), and VAE compression reduces the dimensionality much further, making it easy to reuse image diffusion architectures directly. Mel spectrograms do discard phase information, however, so a high-quality vocoder (such as HiFi-GAN or BigVGAN) is needed to reconstruct the waveform; the fixed window length of the STFT also makes the reconstruction of transient sounds (such as the attack of a percussion note) imprecise.

Diffusion models are also used as vocoders themselves: models such as DiffWave and WaveGrad perform conditional diffusion directly on the waveform, recovering it from a mel spectrogram. In speech synthesis, flow-matching-based non-autoregressive TTS (such as VoiceFlow and Voicebox) has also become one of the mainstream directions.

7.3.3 Audio Editing and Style Transfer

A trained audio diffusion model supports a range of zero-shot editing tasks (the same ideas as SDEdit and inpainting in images; see §4.4):

  • Audio inpainting: mask a specific time-frequency region of the latents and let the model fill in the missing content;
  • Style transfer: noise an existing audio clip to an intermediate time, then denoise it under a new text condition (such as swapping “calm music” for “energetic music”), changing the style while preserving the overall structure;
  • Super-resolution: treat the low-frequency content as known and generate the missing high-frequency detail.

7.4 Discrete-Data Diffusion Models

Standard diffusion models assume data lies in a continuous space $\mathbb{R}^d$ and add Gaussian noise in the forward process. But text, molecular graphs, and categorical data are inherently discrete — “adding 0.1 of Gaussian noise to a word” is meaningless. Extending the diffusion framework to discrete domains requires redefining “noising”: progressively destroying the data with random transitions between states.

7.4.1 Multicategory Discrete Diffusion (D3PM)

D3PM (Discrete Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models; Austin et al., NeurIPS 2021)16 generalizes Gaussian diffusion to discrete state spaces. Suppose each variable has $K$ possible values, represented by a one-hot row vector $x_t$; the forward process implements probabilistic transitions between states through a transition matrix $Q_t$:

$$ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathrm{Cat}\left(x_t;\, p = x_{t-1} Q_t\right) $$

where $[Q_t]_{ij} = q(x_t = j \mid x_{t-1} = i)$, and each row of $Q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ sums to 1. As in the Gaussian case, multiple transitions compose directly, giving closed-form marginals and forward posteriors at any time step:

$$ q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathrm{Cat}\left(x_t;\, p = x_0 \bar{Q}_t\right), \quad \bar{Q}_t = Q_1 Q_2 \cdots Q_t; \qquad q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \mathrm{Cat}\left(x_{t-1};\, p = \frac{x_t Q_t^{\top} \odot x_0 \bar{Q}_{t-1}}{x_0 \bar{Q}_t x_t^{\top}}\right) $$

So during training, $x_t$ can be sampled in one step, while the reverse network learns to predict $x_0$ (or the posterior directly). For multidimensional data $x \in \{1, \dots, K\}^{D}$, each dimension transitions independently:

$$ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \prod_{d=1}^{D} \mathrm{Cat}\left(x_t^{d};\, p = x_{t-1}^{d} Q_t\right) $$

Notation:

SymbolMeaning
$x_t$Discrete state at time step $t$ (one-hot row vector)
$K$Number of categories per dimension
$D$Data dimensionality (e.g., sequence length)
$Q_t$, $\bar{Q}_t$Single-step / cumulative transition probability matrices
$\mathrm{Cat}(\cdot\,; p)$Categorical distribution parameterized by probability vector $p$
$\odot$Elementwise multiplication

D3PM explores several transition matrix designs:

  • Uniform: with some probability, resample the current value uniformly at random to any category;
  • Absorbing: with some probability, transition to a special [MASK] absorbing state; once masked, the value no longer changes, similar to BERT’s masked language modeling;
  • Discretized Gaussian: suited to ordered categories (such as pixel values), favoring transitions to neighboring values to mimic the locality of Gaussian diffusion;
  • Nearest token embedding: define transitions by similarity in the word embedding space, so a word is more likely to become a semantically similar word.

Experiments show that absorbing diffusion performs best on text: starting from a fully masked state, the model progressively “reveals” the original text, an interesting contrast to autoregressive language models.

7.4.2 Autoregressive Diffusion Models (ARDM)

ARDM (Autoregressive Diffusion Models; Hoogeboom et al., ICLR 2022)17 reveals the connection between absorbing diffusion and “order-agnostic autoregressive models” (order-agnostic ARM): in the continuous-time limit, each variable is absorbed into the mask state at some random time, so the forward process is equivalent to choosing a “masking order” uniformly from all permutations of the variables; the reverse process “unmasks” in the opposite order, which amounts to generating variables one by one in random order. ARDM’s advantages are:

  • Parallel generation: unlike standard autoregressive models, which must generate token by token in a fixed order, ARDM can predict multiple not-yet-generated variables in parallel in a single step;
  • Dynamic-programming grouping: dynamic programming can choose the optimal “how many variables to generate per step” for a given step budget, trading off network calls against likelihood, so generation completes in far fewer steps than the data dimensionality;
  • Likelihood: reaches $\le 1.43$ BPC (bits per character, lower is better) on Text8.

7.4.3 Continuous-Time and Masked Diffusion Language Models

SEDD (Score Entropy Discrete Diffusion; Lou et al., ICML 2024) extends the notion of a score to discrete space: it learns the probability ratio $p_t(y)/p_t(x)$ between different states (called a concrete score) and introduces a score entropy loss, substantially narrowing the perplexity gap between diffusion language models and autoregressive models.

MDLM (Masked Diffusion Language Models; Sahoo et al., NeurIPS 2024)18 shows that simple masked diffusion, combined with modern training techniques, is already very powerful. In continuous time, its negative ELBO simplifies to a compact form:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{NELBO}}^{\infty} = \mathbb{E}_{q} \int_{0}^{1} \frac{\alpha_t'}{1 - \alpha_t} \sum_{\ell} \log \left\langle x_\theta^{\ell}(z_t, t),\, x^{\ell} \right\rangle \mathrm{d}t $$
SymbolMeaning
$\alpha_t$Probability that a token is not masked at time $t$, decreasing monotonically from $\alpha_0 \approx 1$ to $\alpha_1 \approx 0$
$\alpha_t'$Derivative of $\alpha_t$ with respect to $t$ (negative, so the overall loss is positive)
$z_t$Partially masked sequence
$x_\theta^{\ell}(z_t, t)$Network’s predicted distribution for the original token at position $\ell$
$\left\langle \cdot, x^{\ell} \right\rangle$Extracts the predicted probability corresponding to the true token $x^{\ell}$ (the sum is nonzero only at masked positions)

Intuition: this objective is “a weighted average of BERT-style masked language modeling losses at different mask rates,” bridging generative diffusion models and encoder-only BERT. Along this line, LLaDA (Nie et al., 2025)19 trained an 8-billion-parameter masked diffusion language model from scratch, performing on par with same-size autoregressive baselines (such as LLaMA3 8B) under a pretraining-plus-supervised-fine-tuning paradigm, and excelling at “reversal curse” tasks (such as completing a poem backward), showing that diffusion models are viable for large-scale language modeling as well.

7.4.4 Continuous Relaxations and Diffusion in Embedding Space

Another strategy leaves Gaussian diffusion itself unchanged and instead diffuses discrete data in a continuous space:

  • Bit Diffusion / Analog Bits (Chen et al., ICLR 2023): represent integer data as binary bits, perform continuous diffusion on the real-valued bits, then threshold back to discrete values after generation;
  • Plaid (Gulrajani & Hashimoto, NeurIPS 2023): map discrete tokens into a continuous embedding space, perform Gaussian diffusion on the embeddings, and narrow the gap to autoregressive models through likelihood training and scaling-law studies;
  • RDLM (Riemannian Diffusion Language Model; Jo & Hwang, NeurIPS 2025)20: exploits the geometry of categorical distributions to define continuous diffusion on a statistical manifold, linking discrete diffusion to continuous flows on manifolds, and uses radial symmetry to enable simulation-free training; it reaches $\le 1.32$ BPC on Text8, surpassing all previous diffusion models.

7.5 Scientific Applications: Molecules, Proteins, and Materials Generation

The use of diffusion models in the natural sciences is growing rapidly, especially when it comes to generating three-dimensional structures that satisfy physical and chemical constraints. Such data shares a common property: the structure is invariant under rotation and translation (and sometimes reflection), so the network usually needs to be E(3) / SE(3) equivariant — rotate the input molecule as a whole, and the predicted noise or velocity should rotate with it in the same way.

7.5.1 Molecule Generation and Structure-Based Drug Design

FlowSBDD (Zhang et al., 2024)21 brings Rectified Flow (§6.5) to structure-based drug design (SBDD): given a protein pocket, generate a 3D ligand molecule that binds to it. The paper notes that conventional diffusion models, constrained by their fixed learning objective, struggle to flexibly incorporate additional optimization targets or conditions; FlowSBDD builds on the Rectified Flow framework (with the time convention of §6.4, where $x_0$ is noise and $x_1$ is the molecule):

$$ \frac{\mathrm{d}x_t}{\mathrm{d}t} = v_\theta\left(x_t, t, c_{\text{pocket}}\right), \quad x_0 \sim p_0, \; x_1 \sim p_1 $$

where $x_t$ is the molecule’s atom coordinates and atom types, $v_\theta$ is the velocity field network, and $c_{\text{pocket}}$ is the protein pocket condition. Because the training objective is simply to regress the velocity along a linear interpolation path, FlowSBDD can:

  • flexibly add extra losses (such as a bond-length loss) to improve chemical validity;
  • take additional conditions as input, or replace the initial Gaussian with another distribution;
  • reach an average Vina Dock score of −8.50 and 75.0% diversity on CrossDocked202021.

Notation:

SymbolMeaning
$x_t$Molecular state at time $t$ (coordinates + atom types)
$v_\theta$Velocity field parameterized by the network
$c_{\text{pocket}}$Conditional features of the protein pocket
$p_0, p_1$Initial distribution (noise) and target distribution (real molecules)

Other representative work includes:

  • EDM (E(3) Equivariant Diffusion Model; Hoogeboom et al., ICML 2022): uses an E(3)-equivariant graph network to denoise atom coordinates (continuous) and atom types simultaneously, generating 3D molecules. Note that it shares its name with, but is completely different from, the EDM of Karras et al. in Chapter 2 (Elucidating the Design space of diffusion Models);
  • GeoDiff (Xu et al., ICLR 2022): given a molecular graph, generates its 3D conformer while guaranteeing rotation-translation invariance;
  • TargetDiff (Guan et al., ICLR 2023): SE(3)-equivariant diffusion under pocket conditioning, jointly generating a ligand’s atom positions and types;
  • DiffSBDD (Schneuing et al., 2022 / 2024)22: E(3)-equivariant 3D conditional diffusion that generates ligand molecules directly in the pocket.

7.5.2 Protein Structure Generation

Protein backbone generation requires outputting an amino-acid chain structure that is physically foldable and designable. The main methods include:

  • ProteinSGM: a score-based generative model that diffuses over inter-residue distance and angle matrices, then rebuilds 3D coordinates with Rosetta;
  • FoldingDiff: represents the protein backbone as a sequence of internal angles (torsion and bond angles) and generates over the angles with DDPM, which is naturally rotation-translation invariant and avoids the complexity of modeling 3D coordinates directly;
  • Chroma (Ingraham et al., Nature 2023): diffuses directly on backbone atom coordinates using correlated noise that follows polymer statistics, and supports a range of composable conditional constraints;
  • RFdiffusion (Watson et al., Nature 2023): a diffusion model fine-tuned from the structure prediction network RoseTTAFold, supporting unconditional backbone generation as well as conditional tasks such as motif scaffolding and binder design, validated by extensive experiments;
  • Protpardelle: an all-atom (including side chains) protein diffusion model that generates sequence and structure together;
  • AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al., Nature 2024): in the structure prediction task, uses a diffusion module to generate all atom coordinates directly, replacing AlphaFold 2’s structure module, and can predict complexes of proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, and more.

7.5.3 Materials Design and Crystal Structure Generation

Applications of diffusion models in materials science include:

  • Crystal structure generation: models such as CDVAE and DiffCSP diffuse under the periodic constraints of the crystal lattice, generating structures with a specified stoichiometric ratio;
  • MatterGen (Zeni et al.)23: proposes a diffusion process that progressively refines atom types, coordinates, and the periodic lattice together, generating stable, diverse inorganic materials spanning the entire periodic table; adapter modules fine-tune it on labeled data to steer generation toward target properties (such as chemical composition, symmetry, magnetism, and electronic and mechanical properties). The paper reports that the fraction of generated structures that are “novel and stable” is more than twice that of previous generative models, and closer to local energy minima;
  • Property-guided generation: use classifier guidance or classifier-free guidance (§3.2–§3.3) to generate new materials with target properties such as a desired band gap or ionic conductivity.

8.3 Evaluation Metrics

Generative models have no “ground truth answer,” so we need a complementary set of metrics to measure fidelity (how much the samples resemble real data), diversity (whether the data distribution is covered), and conditional alignment (whether the prompt is followed).

FID (Fréchet Inception Distance): feed real and generated samples separately through an Inception-v3 network, take their features, fit a Gaussian to each — $\mathcal{N}(\mu_r, \Sigma_r)$ and $\mathcal{N}(\mu_g, \Sigma_g)$ — and compute the Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians:

$$ \mathrm{FID} = \left\lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \right\rVert_2^2 + \operatorname{Tr}\left( \Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2 \left( \Sigma_r \Sigma_g \right)^{1/2} \right) $$

Lower FID is better, and it reflects both fidelity and diversity. It is sensitive to sample count and implementation details, so comparisons must use the same number of samples (commonly 50,000, i.e., FID-50K), the same reference set, and the same evaluation code.

8.4 Common Pitfalls and a Troubleshooting Guide

8.4.1 Unstable Training

Symptoms: loss diverges, NaN gradients appear, and generated samples collapse to a solid color or noise.

Root causes and fixes:

Root causeDiagnosisSolution
Learning rate too highLoss oscillates over the first 1000 steps, then divergesLower the learning rate to around $1 \times 10^{-4}$ and use warmup
Poor noise scheduleLoss stops decreasing late in training, or quality is poor at high / low noise levelsSwitch to a cosine schedule, the EDM framework, or flow matching; check the time-step sampling distribution
Exploding gradientsGradient norm far above normalEnable gradient clipping (max_norm=1.0) and check initialization

Key equation: a common learning rate schedule with warmup (the “inverse square root” schedule from the original Transformer paper); let the learning rate at training step $k$ be:

$$ \mathrm{lr}(k) = \mathrm{lr}_{\text{base}} \cdot \min\left( \frac{k}{k_{\text{warmup}}},\; \left( \frac{k}{k_{\text{warmup}}} \right)^{-1/2} \right) $$

The first $k_{\text{warmup}}$ steps ramp up linearly to $\mathrm{lr}_{\text{base}}$, then decay as $1/\sqrt{k}$. The training script in §8.6.4 uses another equally common schedule, “linear warmup + cosine decay.”

8.4.3 Out-of-Memory (OOM) Errors

Symptoms: CUDA out of memory; training or sampling cannot finish.

Optimization strategies (in recommended order of trying):

  1. Mixed-precision training: FP16 / BF16 roughly halves activation memory (FP16 needs gradient scaling);
  2. Gradient checkpointing: trade time for space, substantially reducing activation memory (commonly 30%~50%, depending on the model);
  3. Lower the batch size: pair it with gradient accumulation to preserve the effective batch size;
  4. Efficient attention: use memory-efficient attention implementations such as FlashAttention / xFormers;
  5. Lower the resolution: downsample images / video / spectrograms, or train at low resolution first and fine-tune at high resolution;
  6. Distributed strategies: for large models, shard parameters and optimizer states with FSDP, DeepSpeed ZeRO, etc.;
  7. Video-specific: reduce the frame count $N_f$ or spatial resolution, or generate long video in segments.

8.4.4 Poor Sampling Quality

Symptoms: blurry generations, structural errors, unreadable text, and flickering video.

Checklist:

  • Are you using the EMA model rather than the raw in-training weights?
  • Is the CFG scale too high (for images, $w > 15$ often causes oversaturation)?
  • Are there enough sampling steps (DDIM usually needs more than 20)?
  • Is conditioning encoded correctly (do the text tokenizer and CLIP / T5 model versions match training)?
  • Does the VAE decoder match (video needs a temporal VAE, or flicker is likely)?
  • Do the noise schedule and prediction type ($\epsilon$ / $v$ / velocity) match training (if training used a linear schedule, you can’t compute coefficients as cosine at sampling time)?
  • Terminal SNR and starting time step: common schedules are not pure noise at $t = T$ (terminal SNR is not zero), and some sampler implementations don’t start from the last time step, causing a train-inference mismatch — for instance, Stable Diffusion struggles to produce very bright or very dark images. Lin et al.24 recommend rescaling the schedule to zero terminal SNR, switching to $v$-prediction, having the sampler always start from the last time step, and rescaling CFG to prevent overexposure;
  • Resolution and noise schedule shift: the higher the resolution, the more correlated neighboring pixels are, and the less information the same noise level “destroys,” so high-resolution models usually need to shift the time-step / noise schedule toward higher noise (such as SD3’s resolution-dependent time-step shift).

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader

class DDPM(nn.Module):
    """
    Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) training framework.
    
    Args:
        nn_model: noise prediction network (e.g., UNet), takes (x_t, t, c) and outputs the predicted noise
        betas: (beta1, beta2) noise schedule range, default (1e-4, 0.02)
        n_T: total number of diffusion steps, default 1000
        drop_prob: conditioning dropout probability during CFG training, default 0.1
    """
    def __init__(self, nn_model, betas=(1e-4, 0.02), n_T=1000, drop_prob=0.1):
        super(DDPM, self).__init__()
        self.nn_model = nn_model
        self.n_T = n_T
        self.drop_prob = drop_prob
        
        # Precompute DDPM schedule parameters
        for k, v in self.ddpm_schedules(betas[0], betas[1], n_T).items():
            self.register_buffer(k, v)  # Register as a buffer — not optimized, but saved with the model
        
        self.loss_mse = nn.MSELoss()
    
    def ddpm_schedules(self, beta1, beta2, T):
        """
        Precompute all intermediate variables needed by DDPM to avoid recomputing them during training.
        
        Args:
            beta1, beta2: start and end values of the noise schedule
            T: total number of diffusion steps
        
        Returns:
            dict: dictionary of tensors such as alpha_t, alphabar_t, sqrtab
        """
        assert beta1 < beta2 < 1.0, "beta must be in (0,1) and monotonically increasing"
        
        beta_t = (beta2 - beta1) * torch.arange(0, T + 1, dtype=torch.float32) / T + beta1
        sqrt_beta_t = torch.sqrt(beta_t)
        alpha_t = 1.0 - beta_t
        
        log_alpha_t = torch.log(alpha_t)
        alphabar_t = torch.cumsum(log_alpha_t, dim=0).exp()  # \bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s
        
        sqrtab = torch.sqrt(alphabar_t)               # \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}
        sqrtmab = torch.sqrt(1.0 - alphabar_t)         # \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}
        oneover_sqrta = 1.0 / torch.sqrt(alpha_t)      # 1 / \sqrt{\alpha_t}
        mab_over_sqrtmab = (1.0 - alpha_t) / sqrtmab # (1 - \alpha_t) / \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}
        
        return {
            "alpha_t": alpha_t,               # \alpha_t
            "oneover_sqrta": oneover_sqrta,   # 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}
            "sqrt_beta_t": sqrt_beta_t,       # \sqrt{\beta_t}
            "alphabar_t": alphabar_t,         # \bar{\alpha}_t
            "sqrtab": sqrtab,                 # \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}
            "sqrtmab": sqrtmab,               # \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}
            "mab_over_sqrtmab": mab_over_sqrtmab,
        }
    
    def forward(self, x, c):
        """
        Forward pass for training. Randomly samples a time step and noise, then computes the prediction loss after noising.
        
        Args:
            x: clean data batch, shape (B, C, H, W)
            c: condition (e.g., class label or text embedding), shape (B, ...)
        
        Returns:
            loss: MSE noise prediction loss
        """
        _ts = torch.randint(1, self.n_T + 1, (x.shape[0],), device=x.device)
        noise = torch.randn_like(x)  # \epsilon ~ N(0, I)
        
        # Forward noising: x_t = sqrt(\bar{\alpha}_t) * x_0 + sqrt(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) * \epsilon
        x_t = self.sqrtab[_ts].view(-1, 1, 1, 1) * x + \
              self.sqrtmab[_ts].view(-1, 1, 1, 1) * noise
        
        # CFG: drop the condition with probability drop_prob (zero it out or replace with a null embedding)
        context_mask = torch.bernoulli(
            torch.zeros(x.shape[0], 1, device=x.device) + self.drop_prob
        )
        c = c * (1.0 - context_mask)  # if dropped, the condition is multiplied by 0
        
        # Predict the noise and compare with the true noise
        noise_pred = self.nn_model(x_t, _ts / self.n_T, c)
        return self.loss_mse(noise, noise_pred)

    @torch.no_grad()
    def sample(self, n_sample, size, device, guide_w=0.0, c=None):
        """
        DDPM sampling with classifier-free guidance (CFG) support.
        
        Args:
            n_sample: number of samples
            size: size of each sample, e.g., (C, H, W)
            device: compute device
            guide_w: CFG guidance strength; 0 means unconditional
            c: condition tensor, shape (n_sample, ...), or None
        
        Returns:
            x_i: generated samples, shape (n_sample, C, H, W)
        """
        # Initialize from pure noise, x_T ~ N(0, I)
        x_i = torch.randn(n_sample, *size, device=device)
        
        if c is None:
            c = torch.zeros(n_sample, 1, device=device)
        else:
            c = c.to(device)
        
        # Prepare doubled conditions for CFG: first half conditional, second half unconditional (null)
        c_i = c.repeat(2, 1)
        context_mask = torch.zeros(2 * n_sample, 1, device=device)
        context_mask[n_sample:] = 1.0  # second half is unconditional
        
        # Denoise step by step from T down to 1
        for i in range(self.n_T, 0, -1):
            t_is = torch.tensor([i / self.n_T], device=device).repeat(2 * n_sample, 1)
            
            # Double the input for efficiency (compute conditional and unconditional in one forward pass)
            x_i_in = x_i.repeat(2, 1, 1, 1)
            noise_pred = self.nn_model(x_i_in, t_is[:, 0], c_i, context_mask)
            
            # Split conditional and unconditional predictions
            eps_cond = noise_pred[:n_sample]      # conditional prediction \epsilon(x_t, t, c)
            eps_uncond = noise_pred[n_sample:]    # unconditional prediction \epsilon(x_t, t, \emptyset)
            
            # CFG formula: \epsilon_guided = \epsilon_uncond + w * (\epsilon_cond - \epsilon_uncond)
            # Equivalent to: (1 + w) * \epsilon_cond - w * \epsilon_uncond
            eps = eps_uncond + guide_w * (eps_cond - eps_uncond)
            
            # DDPM reverse sampling update (simplified, no learned variance)
            x_i = self.oneover_sqrta[i] * (x_i - eps * self.mab_over_sqrtmab[i])
            
            # Add random noise (not on the last step)
            if i > 1:
                z = torch.randn_like(x_i)
                x_i = x_i + self.sqrt_beta_t[i] * z
        
        return x_i

Warning

The code uses $\hat{\epsilon} = \epsilon_{\text{uncond}} + w\left(\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \epsilon_{\text{uncond}}\right)$, which expands to $w\, \epsilon_{\text{cond}} + (1 - w)\, \epsilon_{\text{uncond}}$. The comment’s claim that this is “equivalent to $(1 + w)\, \epsilon_{\text{cond}} - w\, \epsilon_{\text{uncond}}$” is false — the latter corresponds to the other convention $\epsilon_{\text{cond}} + w\left(\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \epsilon_{\text{uncond}}\right)$ (see the note in §8.2.2). Under this code’s convention, guide_w=0 is purely unconditional generation, guide_w=1 is ordinary conditional generation, and only guide_w>1 gives enhanced guidance.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class FlowMatchingTrainer(nn.Module):
    """
    Flow matching trainer (Rectified Flow / OT-CFM).
    
    Assume the data distribution is p_1 (clean data) and the prior is p_0 (standard Gaussian noise).
    Build a linear interpolation between x_0 and x_1: x_t = (1-t) * x_0 + t * x_1
    The network learns the velocity field v_theta(x_t, t, c), aiming to match the true velocity x_1 - x_0.
    """
    def __init__(self, velocity_net, sigma_min=1e-4):
        super().__init__()
        self.velocity_net = velocity_net
        self.sigma_min = sigma_min  # minimum noise level, for numerical stability
    
    def forward(self, x1, c):
        """
        Flow Matching training forward pass.
        
        Args:
            x1: clean data, shape (B, C, H, W)
            c: condition
        
        Returns:
            loss: mean squared velocity matching loss
        """
        B = x1.shape[0]
        device = x1.device
        
        # Sample x_0 ~ N(0, I) from the prior
        x0 = torch.randn_like(x1)
        
        # Sample a time step t ~ Uniform(0, 1)
        t = torch.rand(B, device=device) * (1 - self.sigma_min) + self.sigma_min
        
        # Linear interpolation to construct x_t = (1-t) * x_0 + t * x_1
        # note that t must be broadcast to the data dimensions
        t_expanded = t.view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
        xt = (1 - t_expanded) * x0 + t_expanded * x1
        
        # True velocity field: dx_t/dt = x_1 - x_0
        target_velocity = x1 - x0
        
        # Network's predicted velocity field
        pred_velocity = self.velocity_net(xt, t, c)
        
        # Loss: difference between predicted and target velocities
        loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(pred_velocity, target_velocity)
        return loss

    @torch.no_grad()
    def sample(self, n_sample, size, device, c=None, n_steps=50, cfg_scale=1.0):
        """
        Sample a Flow Matching model using Euler integration.
        
        Args:
            n_sample: number of samples
            size: data dimensions
            device: device
            c: condition
            n_steps: number of ODE integration steps (n_steps=1 means single-step generation)
            cfg_scale: CFG strength
        
        Returns:
            x: generated samples
        """
        # Initialize x_0 ~ N(0, I)
        x = torch.randn(n_sample, *size, device=device)
        
        if c is not None and cfg_scale != 1.0:
            # CFG: merge conditional and unconditional inputs
            c_in = c.repeat(2, 1) if c.dim() == 2 else c.repeat(2)
            context_mask = torch.zeros(2 * n_sample, device=device)
            context_mask[n_sample:] = 1.0
        else:
            c_in = c
            context_mask = None
        
        dt = 1.0 / n_steps
        
        for i in range(n_steps):
            t = torch.ones(n_sample, device=device) * (i * dt)
            
            if c_in is not None and cfg_scale != 1.0:
                x_in = x.repeat(2, 1, 1, 1)
                t_in = t.repeat(2)
                v_pred = self.velocity_net(x_in, t_in, c_in, context_mask)
                v_cond = v_pred[:n_sample]
                v_uncond = v_pred[n_sample:]
                v = v_uncond + cfg_scale * (v_cond - v_uncond)
            else:
                v = self.velocity_net(x, t, c_in)
            
            # Euler step: x_{t+dt} = x_t + dt * v(x_t, t)
            x = x + dt * v
        
        return x

"""
A runnable, complete training script framework (needs a dataset and model definitions).

import torch
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torch.optim import AdamW
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import LambdaLR
import math

def train_diffusion_model(
    model,
    dataset,
    n_epochs=100,
    batch_size=128,
    lr=1e-4,
    warmup_steps=5000,
    device="cuda",
    use_amp=True,
    grad_clip=1.0,
    save_every=10,
):
    """
    General diffusion model training loop.
    
    Args:
        model: DDPM or FlowMatching training module
        dataset: PyTorch Dataset
        n_epochs: number of epochs
        batch_size: batch size
        lr: base learning rate
        warmup_steps: number of warmup steps
        device: training device
        use_amp: whether to use mixed precision
        grad_clip: gradient clipping threshold
        save_every: save every N epochs
    """
    dataloader = DataLoader(
        dataset, batch_size=batch_size, shuffle=True, 
        num_workers=4, pin_memory=True, drop_last=True
    )
    
    model = model.to(device)
    optimizer = AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr, weight_decay=0.01)
    
    # Learning rate warmup + cosine decay
    def lr_lambda(step):
        if step < warmup_steps:
            return step / warmup_steps
        else:
            progress = (step - warmup_steps) / max(1, len(dataloader) * n_epochs - warmup_steps)
            return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))
    
    scheduler = LambdaLR(optimizer, lr_lambda)
    
    # Mixed-precision training
    scaler = torch.cuda.amp.GradScaler() if use_amp else None
    
    model.train()
    global_step = 0
    
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        epoch_loss = 0.0
        
        for batch_idx, (x, c) in enumerate(dataloader):
            x, c = x.to(device), c.to(device)
            
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            
            if use_amp:
                with torch.cuda.amp.autocast():
                    loss = model(x, c)
                scaler.scale(loss).backward()
                scaler.unscale_(optimizer)
                torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), grad_clip)
                scaler.step(optimizer)
                scaler.update()
            else:
                loss = model(x, c)
                loss.backward()
                torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), grad_clip)
                optimizer.step()
            
            scheduler.step()
            global_step += 1
            epoch_loss += loss.item()
            
            if batch_idx % 100 == 0:
                print(f"Epoch {epoch}, Step {global_step}, Loss: {loss.item():.4f}, LR: {scheduler.get_last_lr()[0]:.6f}")
        
        avg_loss = epoch_loss / len(dataloader)
        print(f"=== Epoch {epoch} completed. Average Loss: {avg_loss:.4f} ===")
        
        if (epoch + 1) % save_every == 0:
            torch.save({
                'epoch': epoch,
                'model_state_dict': model.state_dict(),
                'optimizer_state_dict': optimizer.state_dict(),
                'scheduler_state_dict': scheduler.state_dict(),
                'loss': avg_loss,
            }, f"checkpoint_epoch_{epoch}.pt")
    
    return model

Tip

This framework omits EMA. In real training, maintain an extra set of EMA weights (updated after every optimizer.step() using the formula in §8.2.1), and use the EMA weights both when saving checkpoints and when sampling; PyTorch’s torch.optim.swa_utils.AveragedModel or diffusers’ EMAModel can be used directly.

8.7 A Quality Checklist

Before deploying a diffusion model, you can use the following checklist to verify that the implementation is correct:

  • Correct equations: is the training objective $\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta \rVert^2$ (DDPM noise prediction) or $\lVert v - v_\theta \rVert^2$ (Flow Matching)? Is the time-direction convention (which end is the data) consistent throughout?
  • Notation consistency: is every variable in every equation defined in the text?
  • CFG implementation: are conditions dropped with about 10% probability during training? Does sampling compute conditional and unconditional predictions together, and does the guidance formula match the convention used for $w$?
  • EMA usage: is an EMA model maintained during training? Are EMA weights used for sampling?
  • Noise schedule: do training and sampling use the same noise schedule? When switching between DDIM / DDPM, do the computations of $\alpha$ and $\bar{\alpha}$ match?
  • Time encoding: is the time step $t$ fed to the network the same way as in training (e.g., $t/T$ or continuous time)?
  • Gradient checks: is gradient clipping enabled? Is FP16 mixed precision paired with GradScaler?
  • Dimension checks: do the broadcast dimensions of the condition embedding match the data dimensions?
  • Evaluation: do metrics such as FID use a fixed sample count, reference set, and evaluation code?
  • Reproducibility test: with the random seed fixed, does the same input produce the same output?
  • VRAM monitoring: does the training script log peak memory usage?
