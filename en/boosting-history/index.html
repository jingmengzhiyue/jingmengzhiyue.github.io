2.1 The core idea

The core idea of boosting fits in one sentence: combine, in sequence, a series of weak learners that are only slightly better than random guessing, and build a strong learner of arbitrarily high accuracy.

That’s a very different philosophy from bagging’s parallel voting. If bagging is many minds pulling together while staying diverse, boosting is steady, step-by-step refinement. Each round tries to correct what the previous model got wrong, concentrating on the samples that are hardest to learn. This adaptive strategy lets boosting approach the target function gradually, from simple to complex, from rough to fine.

Mathematically, the ultimate goal is to learn a function $F(x)$ that minimizes some loss:

$$F^*(x) = \arg\min_F \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\left[L(y, F(x))\right]$$

In practice we can only minimize the empirical loss on the training set, $\sum_{i=1}^N L(y_i, F(x_i))$. Solving that optimization over the whole function space is usually intractable, so boosting takes a greedy, incremental approach: at round $m$ we already have a model $F_{m-1}(x)$, and we add a new base learner $h_m(x)$ with weight $\beta_m$ so that the updated model $F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \beta_m h_m(x)$ drives the loss down further.

2.2 Three key mechanisms

Boosting’s jump from weak learner to strong learner rests on three interlocking mechanisms.

2.2.1 Adaptive reweighting

Adaptive reweighting is what sets boosting apart from other ensemble methods. Each round, the algorithm adjusts the weights of the training samples according to how the earlier models performed: misclassified samples get more weight, correctly classified ones get less. That forces later models to pay attention to the hard cases.

In AdaBoost, for example, the sample weights update as

$$w_i^{(m+1)} = \frac{w_i^{(m)} \exp\left(-\alpha_m y_i G_m(x_i)\right)}{Z_m}$$

where $Z_m$ is a normalization factor that keeps the weights summing to 1. When $y_i \neq G_m(x_i)$, we have $y_i G_m(x_i) = -1$, the exponential becomes $\exp(\alpha_m) > 1$, and the weight goes up. When $y_i = G_m(x_i)$, the weight goes down.

(The full derivation of AdaBoost’s weight update and of $\alpha_m$ is in Chapter 4.)

This adaptive mechanism lets boosting shift its focus dynamically and spend its effort where the model is currently weakest. As MDPI Energies (2026) puts it: “The core idea of AdaBoost is to iteratively train a series of weak learners, each focusing on the samples that the previous learner misclassified.”

2.2.2 Sequential error correction

Sequential error correction is the second mechanism. Each new base learner $h_m(x)$ isn’t trained independently; it’s designed to make up for what the current ensemble lacks. In function space, that amounts to taking a step in some direction — usually the negative gradient — to get closer to the optimum.

In gradient boosting, this correction is formalized as fitting the negative gradient (pseudo-residual):

$$r_{im} = -\left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F=F_{m-1}}$$

The new base learner $h_m(x)$ is trained to fit these negative gradients, which is to say it learns where, and on which samples, the current model most needs improvement. The model then updates as

$$F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \rho_m h_m(x)$$

where the step size $\rho_m$ usually comes from a line search. This cycle — fit the residual, update the model, fit the new residual — brings the training loss down step by step. (In practice a learning rate and early stopping are used, and the goal isn’t to converge on the training set; see Chapter 5.)

2.2.3 Weighted majority vote

The final prediction in boosting is a weighted combination rather than a plain average. Each base learner’s weight $\alpha_m$ reflects its performance: more accurate learners get more say, less accurate ones get less. For classification, the combined classifier is

$$G(x) = \operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_{m=1}^M \alpha_m G_m(x)\right)$$

Weighting ensures that high-quality base learners carry more influence over the final decision, while poor ones aren’t entirely ignored — they may do well in some regions. In AdaBoost, as the weighted error rate approaches $1/2$ (random guessing), $\alpha_m$ approaches 0.

“Boosting is a technique that involves creating a sequence of weak learners, each one focusing on the instances that the previous learner misclassified. The final model is a weighted combination of these weak learners, where the weights are determined by the accuracy of each learner.” — GitHub Machine Learning xbeat

2.3 Weak and strong learners in PAC learning theory

Boosting’s theoretical foundation sits on the PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) framework of computational learning theory. Valiant (1984) introduced the PAC framework in “A Theory of the Learnable”; Kearns & Valiant (technical report 1988, STOC 1989) introduced weak and strong learners; and Schapire (1990) proved the two are equivalent.

2.3.1 Weak learner

A weak learning algorithm (or $\gamma$-weak learner) is one for which there exists a fixed $\gamma > 0$ such that for any distribution $\mathcal{D}$, any target concept and any $\delta \in (0, 1)$, given $m(\delta)$ i.i.d. samples from $\mathcal{D}$ it produces, with probability at least $1-\delta$, a hypothesis $h_S \in \mathcal{H}$ whose generalization error satisfies

$$\operatorname{er}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \leq \frac{1}{2} - \gamma$$

Here $\gamma > 0$ is the weak learner’s advantage (edge), the amount by which it beats random guessing (error $1/2$). See Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David 2014, Definition 10.1.

Two points deserve emphasis:

  • “Any distribution” is the crux. A weak learner has to do slightly better than random guessing on every input distribution $\mathcal{D}$, not just on one particular training distribution. Boosting exploits exactly this: it keeps constructing new (reweighted) distributions, calls the weak learner on each, and gets back a hypothesis better than chance every time.
  • In the classical PAC definition a weak learner must also run in polynomial time, and the advantage $\gamma$ can be as small as $1/\operatorname{poly}(n)$.

“A weak learning algorithm runs in polynomial time and outputs a hypothesis that is probably barely correct, meaning that its accuracy is slightly better than the strategy that randomly guesses the label of an instance by predicting 1 with probability 1/2 and 0 with probability 1/2.” — MIT LCS-TR-811

In practice the weak learner is usually something simple:

  • Decision stumps: a tree with a single split, dividing the data into at most two regions.
  • Shallow decision trees: trees limited to depth 3-5.
  • Simple linear models: linear discriminant analysis, for instance.

“By default, AdaBoost uses Decision Tree with max_depth = 1 which is also known as stump as the individual weak learner. A stump is a decision tree with a root node and two leaf nodes.” — Towards Data Science, 2025

2.3.2 Strong learner

A strong learning algorithm — a PAC learning algorithm in the usual sense — is one that, for any distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and any $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ with $0 < \varepsilon, \delta < 1$, given $m(\varepsilon, \delta)$ i.i.d. samples ($m$ polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$ and $1/\delta$) produces, with probability at least $1-\delta$, a hypothesis $h_S$ whose generalization error satisfies

$$\operatorname{er}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \leq \varepsilon$$

In other words, a strong learner reaches arbitrarily high accuracy with arbitrarily high confidence in polynomial time. That’s a much stricter demand than beating random guessing: the learner has to hit any target precision.

2.3.3 From weak to strong: the theoretical breakthrough

The question Kearns & Valiant (1989) raised was: is weak learning equivalent to strong learning? If an algorithm can learn with accuracy slightly above chance, does that mean we can also build one that learns to any accuracy we like?

Schapire (1990) answered yes in “The Strength of Weak Learnability.” He proved that weak and strong learners are equivalent in the PAC framework, and gave the first algorithm that boosts a weak learner into a strong one — the theoretical origin of boosting. The result caused a stir, because it showed that even a barely usable learning algorithm can be turned into an arbitrarily powerful one through a clever combination strategy.

2.3.4 The first boosting algorithm: majority vote over three hypotheses

Schapire’s (1990) construction is recursive. Suppose the weak learner’s error on any distribution is at most $\epsilon < 1/2$:

  1. Train on the original distribution $\mathcal{D}$ to get $h_1$.
  2. Construct a new distribution $\mathcal{D}_2$ on which $h_1$ is exactly half right and half wrong (error rate $1/2$), and train on it to get $h_2$.
  3. Construct a distribution $\mathcal{D}_3$ containing only the samples where $h_1$ and $h_2$ disagree, and train on it to get $h_3$.
  4. Output the majority vote $\operatorname{maj}(h_1, h_2, h_3)$.

Schapire showed that the combined error rate is at most

$$g(\epsilon) = 3\epsilon^2 - 2\epsilon^3$$

Since $g(\epsilon) - \epsilon = -\epsilon(1 - \epsilon)(1 - 2\epsilon) < 0$ for $0 < \epsilon < 1/2$, the combined error is strictly below $\epsilon$. Apply the procedure recursively and the error can be pushed as low as you like, which gives a strong learner. The algorithm is theoretically complete, but the recursion is intricate and it needs to be able to filter samples from a specified distribution, so it has limited practical use.

“Kearns and Valiant raised the question of whether or not a weak learning algorithm could be converted into a strong learning algorithm. They referred to this problem as the hypothesis boosting problem… Thus it came as a great surprise when Robert E. Schapire demonstrated that strong and weak learning actually are equivalent by providing an algorithm for converting a weak learner into strong learner.” — MIT LCS-TR-811

2.4 Four stages in the history of boosting

The history of boosting is a case study in machine learning moving from theory to practice. From the theoretical conjecture of 1988 to today’s widespread industrial use of XGBoost and LightGBM, it has passed through four main stages.

Stage one: theoretical origins (1984-1990)

  • 1984: Valiant publishes “A Theory of the Learnable,” introducing the PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) framework.
  • 1988-1989: Kearns & Valiant introduce weak and strong learning within PAC learning and ask whether the two are equivalent (the “hypothesis boosting problem”). The question laid the theoretical groundwork for boosting.
  • 1990: Schapire publishes “The Strength of Weak Learnability” (Machine Learning, 1990), proving for the first time that a weak learner can be boosted into a strong one and giving the first boosting algorithm (see Section 2.3.4). The original algorithm is computationally inefficient, but it settled the central theoretical question.
  • 1990: Freund proposes Boost-by-Majority (BBM) at COLT 1990, taking a majority vote over a large number of weak hypotheses at once, which is markedly more efficient than Schapire’s recursive construction (journal version in Information and Computation, 1995). BBM’s drawback is that it needs the weak learner’s advantage $\gamma$ known in advance.

“The origins of the boosting method for designing learning machines are traced back to the work of Valiant and Kearns, who posed the question of whether a weak learning algorithm, meaning one that does slightly better than random guessing, can be boosted into a strong one with a good performance index.” — ScienceDirect, Boosting Technique Overview

Stage two: the AdaBoost era (1995-1997)

  • 1995: Freund & Schapire propose AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) at EuroCOLT 1995; the full journal version, “A Decision-Theoretic Generalization of On-Line Learning and an Application to Boosting,” appears in the Journal of Computer and System Sciences in 1997. AdaBoost drops the earlier requirement that the weak learner’s error bound (the advantage $\gamma$) be known in advance, and adapts sample weights instead, which is what made the algorithm practical.
  • 1996: Freund & Schapire publish “Experiments with a New Boosting Algorithm” at ICML 1996, validating AdaBoost on a large number of datasets.
  • AdaBoost’s central contribution: it ties the sample-weight update to classifier performance, and proves that under the weak learning assumption the training error falls exponentially (bounded by the product of the per-round normalization factors, $\prod_m Z_m$). The reading of AdaBoost as minimizing exponential loss came later, from Breiman, Friedman and others working from a statistical angle (see stage three); it wasn’t where the original paper started.
  • 1998: Schapire, Freund, Bartlett & Lee publish “Boosting the Margin” (The Annals of Statistics), using margin theory to explain why AdaBoost’s test error keeps falling even after training error reaches zero (see Chapter 4).
  • 1999: Schapire & Singer publish “Improved Boosting Algorithms Using Confidence-rated Predictions,” extending AdaBoost to weak learners that output real-valued confidence (the basis of Real AdaBoost).
  • 2003: Freund and Schapire receive the Gödel Prize in theoretical computer science for their 1997 AdaBoost paper.

“Freund (1995) proposed a ‘boost by majority’ variation which combined many weak learners simultaneously and improved the performance of the simple boosting algorithm of Schapire. These algorithms then evolved into more adaptive and practical versions such as AdaBoost, where this assumption was dropped.” — Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2000, The Annals of Statistics

The AdaBoost procedure:

  1. Initialize sample weights: $w_i = \frac{1}{N}, \quad i = 1, 2, \ldots, N$
  2. For $m = 1$ to $M$ do:
    • Train a weak classifier $G_m(x)$ under the weights $w_i$
    • Compute the weighted error rate (weights already normalized): $\epsilon_m = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \, \mathbb{I}\left(y_i \neq G_m(x_i)\right)$
    • With $y_i, G_m(x_i) \in \{-1,+1\}$, compute the classifier weight: $$\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1 - \epsilon_m}{\epsilon_m}$$
    • Update the sample weights: $$w_i \leftarrow w_i \cdot \exp\left(-\alpha_m y_i G_m(x_i)\right)$$ then normalize so that $\sum_i w_i = 1$
  3. Output the final classifier: $G(x) = \operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_{m=1}^M \alpha_m G_m(x)\right)$

Here $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

A note on notation: some texts (ESL Algorithm 10.1, for instance) multiply the weight of misclassified samples by $\exp(\alpha_m)$ and leave correct samples alone, in which case $\alpha_m=\ln\left(\frac{1-\epsilon_m}{\epsilon_m}\right)$. Since the weights are normalized every round, and scaling all $\alpha_m$ by 2 doesn’t change the output of $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)$, this is fully equivalent to the $\frac{1}{2}\ln$ version above. What follows uses $\{-1,+1\}$ labels and the $\frac{1}{2}\ln$ form throughout, matching Li Hang’s Statistical Learning Methods.

Stage three: the statistical view and gradient boosting (1997-2003)

  • 1997-1999: In a series of papers and technical reports (“Arcing Classifiers,” The Annals of Statistics, 1998; “Prediction Games and Arcing Algorithms,” Neural Computation, 1999), Breiman makes the breakthrough observation that AdaBoost can be read as a gradient-descent-type algorithm in function space. That moves boosting from an algorithmic trick to a topic in statistical optimization theory.
  • 1999: Mason, Baxter, Bartlett & Frean publish “Boosting Algorithms as Gradient Descent” at NIPS 1999, proposing the AnyBoost framework, which unifies several boosting algorithms as gradient descent on an arbitrary cost functional in function space.
  • 2000: Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani publish “Additive Logistic Regression: A Statistical View of Boosting” (in The Annals of Statistics), reinterpreting AdaBoost as a forward stagewise additive model under exponential loss. This paper is a landmark in the theory of boosting.
  • 2001: Friedman publishes “Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine” (technical report 1999, formally in The Annals of Statistics in 2001), introducing the Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) and extending boosting to any differentiable loss function. The key move is to use the negative gradient of the loss to set each round’s direction, which unifies the regression and classification versions of boosting.
  • 2002: Friedman proposes Stochastic Gradient Boosting, drawing a random subsample of the training data without replacement in each iteration to fit the new tree, adding randomness that reduces variance and speeds up computation (see Chapter 5).
  • 2003: Bühlmann & Yu study boosting under squared loss ($L_2$Boosting) systematically, analyzing its bias-variance trade-off and the role of early stopping.

“Later on, Breiman (1997, 1998, 1999, 2000, 2004) made in a series of papers and technical reports the breakthrough observation that AdaBoost is in fact a gradient-descent-type algorithm in a function space… Friedman et al. (2000), who rederived AdaBoost as a method for fitting an additive model in a forward stagewise manner. Following this, Friedman (2001, 2002) developed a general statistical framework.” — Biau & Cadre, “Optimization by Gradient Boosting” (arXiv:1707.05023, in Advances in Contemporary Statistics and Econometrics)

The core gradient boosting update:

$$F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \rho_m h_m(x)$$

where $h_m(x)$ is fitted to the negative gradient (pseudo-residual):

$$r_{im} = -\left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F=F_{m-1}}$$

Stage four: modern high-performance implementations (2014-present)

  • 2016: Chen & Guestrin introduce XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) at KDD. XGBoost adds a regularization term (L1/L2), a second-order Taylor approximation, an optimized split-finding algorithm, sparsity awareness and parallel computation, and quickly became standard equipment in data science competitions.
  • 2017: Microsoft introduces LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine), built on histogram binning, leaf-wise tree growth, Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB), for a large speedup on big datasets.
  • 2017: Yandex introduces CatBoost (Categorical Boosting, arXiv 2017, published at NeurIPS 2018), aimed at categorical features: Ordered Target Statistics and Ordered Boosting mitigate the prediction shift caused by target leakage, and oblivious (symmetric) trees serve as base learners to speed up prediction and reduce overfitting.

“Recently, the interest in boosting algorithms from a predictive performance perspective has been revived. This revival has been made possible by the introduction of the Extreme Gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm. Following the success of XGBoost, several new competitive algorithms… were introduced. Such algorithms are Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) and Accelerated Gradient Boosting (AGB).” — Tyralis & Papacharalampous, 2021, Neural Computing and Applications

2.6 Chapter summary

This article started from the core idea of boosting and covered two things in detail: the three mechanisms of adaptive reweighting, sequential error correction and weighted majority voting, and the PAC-learning definitions of weak and strong learners. Tracing the four stages from 1984 to today, we saw boosting begin as a purely theoretical question, become practical with AdaBoost, deepen through the statistical view, and end up as modern tools such as XGBoost and LightGBM. The arc is a lesson in machine learning theory as much as in the scientific method of moving from theory to practice.
