Pandas Time Series Data

1
2

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

I. Basic Objects in Time Series

Time series are a familiar concept in daily life, but a concrete temporal event can be described from several different object perspectives. Suppose you must be in class at 8 a.m. on Monday, September 7, 2020, and the class ends at 10 a.m. that same morning. What time concepts does this involve?

  • First, there are timestamps (Date times): the two points ‘2020-9-7 08:00:00’ and ‘2020-9-7 10:00:00’ represent the moments when class begins and ends; in pandas they are called Timestamp. A series of timestamps can form a DatetimeIndex, and once placed in a Series, the Series has dtype datetime64[ns], or datetime64[ns, tz] when a time zone is involved, where tz is short for timezone.

  • Second, there are time deltas (Time deltas): the duration of the class. Subtracting two Timestamp objects gives a time delta, represented in pandas by Timedelta. Likewise, a series of time deltas forms a TimedeltaIndex, and placing it in a Series changes the Series dtype to timedelta64[ns].

  • Third, there are time spans (Time spans): class continues throughout the interval from 8 to 10, represented in pandas by Period. Similarly, a series of time spans forms a PeriodIndex, and placing it in a Series changes the Series dtype to Period.

  • Fourth, there are date offsets (Date offsets): suppose you only know that class starts at 8 a.m. on the first Monday of September, without knowing the exact date; a type is needed for such cases. Again, if you want the 30th working day after September 7, 2020, a time delta cannot solve the problem, and so DateOffset appears in pandas. pandas has no dedicated storage type for a column of date offsets, for a simple reason: such needs are unusual, and generally we only need to apply one uniform special offset to a batch of time features.

This simple example makes it easy to summarize the table from the official documentation:

ConceptSingle-element typeArray typepandas dtype
Date timesTimestampDatetimeIndexdatetime64[ns]
Time deltasTimedeltaTimedeltaIndextimedelta64[ns]
Time spansPeriodPeriodIndexperiod[freq]
Date offsetsDateOffsetNoneNone

Because the time-span objects Period/PeriodIndex are not used very often, they will not be covered here; the discussion is confined to timestamp series, time-delta series, and date offsets.

II. Timestamps

1. Constructing and Attributes of Timestamp

A single timestamp is created with pd.Timestamp; in general, common date formats can all be converted successfully:

1
2

ts = pd.Timestamp('2020/1/1')
ts

Timestamp('2020-01-01 00:00:00')

1
2

ts = pd.Timestamp('2020-1-1 08:10:30')
ts

Timestamp('2020-01-01 08:10:30')

The individual values can be obtained through year, month, day, hour, min, second:

1

ts.year

2020

1

ts.month

1

1

ts.day

1

1

ts.hour

8

1

ts.minute

10

1

ts.second

30

In pandas, the smallest precision of a timestamp is the nanosecond ns; using 64-bit storage, the representable time range can be calculated approximately as follows:

$$\rm Time\,Range = \frac{2^{64}}{10^9\times 60\times 60\times 24\times 365} \approx 585 (Years)$$

The range representable by timestamps can be obtained via pd.Timestamp.max and pd.Timestamp.min; as you can see, the span in years is indeed about the result computed above:

1

pd.Timestamp.max

Timestamp('2262-04-11 23:47:16.854775807')

1

pd.Timestamp.min

Timestamp('1677-09-21 00:12:43.145225')

1

pd.Timestamp.max.year - pd.Timestamp.min.year

585

2. Generating Datetime Series

A group of timestamps can form a time series, generated with to_datetime and date_range. to_datetime converts an object column of timestamp-formatted values into a datetime64[ns] time series:

1
2
3
4

pd.to_datetime(['2020-1-1', '2020-1-3', '2020-1-6'])
df = pd.read_csv('../data/learn_pandas.csv')
s = pd.to_datetime(df.Test_Date)
s.head()

0   2019-10-05
1   2019-09-04
2   2019-09-12
3   2020-01-03
4   2019-11-06
Name: Test_Date, dtype: datetime64[ns]

In the rare cases where the timestamp format does not match, you can force a match with format:

1
2

temp = pd.to_datetime(['2020\\1\\1','2020\\1\\3'],format='%Y\\%m\\%d')
temp

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-01-03'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq=None)

Note that because a list rather than a pandas Series was passed above, the result is a DatetimeIndex; to obtain a datetime64[ns] series, convert explicitly with Series:

1

pd.Series(temp).head()

0   2020-01-01
1   2020-01-03
dtype: datetime64[ns]

There is also a to_datetime operation that concatenates several time columns of a table into a time series; the column names must match the following time-keyword names:

1
2
3
4
5
6
7

df_date_cols = pd.DataFrame({'year': [2020, 2020],
                             'month': [1, 1],
                             'day': [1, 2],
                             'hour': [10, 20],
                             'minute': [30, 50],
                             'second': [20, 40]})
pd.to_datetime(df_date_cols)

0   2020-01-01 10:30:20
1   2020-01-02 20:50:40
dtype: datetime64[ns]

date_range generates a continuous, regularly spaced sequence of times. Its important parameters are start, end, freq, periods, denoting the start time, end time, interval, and number of timestamps. When any three of the four are given, the remaining one is determined. Note that if the start or end date is an endpoint, it is included:

1

pd.date_range('2020-1-1','2020-1-21', freq='10D') # # Inclusive

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-01-11', '2020-01-21'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='10D')

1

pd.date_range('2020-1-1','2020-2-28', freq='10D')

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-01-11', '2020-01-21', '2020-01-31',
               '2020-02-10', '2020-02-20'],
              dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='10D')

1

pd.date_range('2020-1-1', '2020-2-28', periods=6) # # The end date cannot be hit exactly, so freq is not 10 days

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01 00:00:00', '2020-01-12 14:24:00',
               '2020-01-24 04:48:00', '2020-02-04 19:12:00',
               '2020-02-16 09:36:00', '2020-02-28 00:00:00'],
              dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq=None)

The freq parameter here is closely tied to DateOffset objects; its concrete use will be introduced in Section IV.

3. The dt Object

Just as cat, str are defined on category, string series for categorical and text data, a dt object is defined on time-series-typed series to support many time-series operations. For datetime64[ns], these operations fall roughly into three groups: extracting time-related attributes, testing whether timestamps satisfy conditions, and rounding.

The common attributes of the first group include date, time, year, month, day, hour, minute, second, microsecond, nanosecond, dayofweek, dayofyear, weekofyear, daysinmonth, quarter, where daysinmonth, quarter denote the number of days in the month and the quarter respectively.

dtype: int64

1

s.dt.daysinmonth

III. Time Deltas

1. Generating Timedeltas

As noted in Section I, a time delta can be understood as the difference between two timestamps, and it can also be constructed with pd.Timedelta:

1

pd.Timestamp('20200102 08:00:00')-pd.Timestamp('20200101 07:35:00')

Timedelta('1 days 00:25:00')

1

pd.Timedelta(days=1, minutes=25) # # Note the plural "days"

Timedelta('1 days 00:25:00')

1

pd.Timedelta('1 days 25 minutes') # # Built from a string

Timedelta('1 days 00:25:00')

The main way to generate a time-delta series is pd.to_timedelta, whose dtype is timedelta64[ns]:

1
2

s = pd.to_timedelta(df.Time_Record)
s.head()

0   0 days 00:04:34
1   0 days 00:04:20
2   0 days 00:05:22
3   0 days 00:04:08
4   0 days 00:05:22
Name: Time_Record, dtype: timedelta64[ns]

Like date_range, a time-delta series can also be generated with timedelta_range; the two have identical parameters:

1

pd.timedelta_range('0s', '1000s', freq='6min')

TimedeltaIndex(['0 days 00:00:00', '0 days 00:06:00', '0 days 00:12:00'], dtype='timedelta64[ns]', freq='6T')

1

pd.timedelta_range('0s', '1000s', periods=3)

TimedeltaIndex(['0 days 00:00:00', '0 days 00:08:20', '0 days 00:16:40'], dtype='timedelta64[ns]', freq=None)

As with timestamp series, a dt object is also defined for Timedelta series. The attributes defined above include days, seconds, mircroseconds, nanoseconds, which return the corresponding features of the time delta. Note that seconds here does not mean the total number of seconds, but the seconds left after taking the remainder over days:

1

s.dt.seconds.head()

0    274
1    260
2    322
3    248
4    322
Name: Time_Record, dtype: int64

If you want the total seconds rather than the remainder over days, use total_seconds

1

s.dt.total_seconds().head()

0    274.0
1    260.0
2    322.0
3    248.0
4    322.0
Name: Time_Record, dtype: float64

As with timestamp series, the rounding functions can also be used on the dt object:

1

pd.to_timedelta(df.Time_Record).dt.round('min').head()

0   0 days 00:05:00
1   0 days 00:04:00
2   0 days 00:05:00
3   0 days 00:04:00
4   0 days 00:05:00
Name: Time_Record, dtype: timedelta64[ns]

Time deltas support three common kinds of operations: multiplication by a scalar, addition and subtraction with timestamps, and addition, subtraction, and division with other time deltas:

1
2
3
4

td1 = pd.Timedelta(days=1)
td2 = pd.Timedelta(days=3)
ts = pd.Timestamp('20200101')
td1 * 2

Timedelta('2 days 00:00:00')

1

td2 - td1

Timedelta('2 days 00:00:00')

1

ts + td1

Timestamp('2020-01-02 00:00:00')

1

ts - td1

Timestamp('2019-12-31 00:00:00')

All of these operations carry over to time-delta series:

td2 = pd.timedelta_range(start='12 hours', freq='2H', periods=5)
ts = pd.date_range('20200101', '20200105')

TimedeltaIndex(['5 days', '10 days', '15 days', '20 days', '25 days'], dtype='timedelta64[ns]', freq='5D')

td1 * pd.Series(list(range(5))) # # Element-wise multiplication

3   12 days
4   20 days
dtype: timedelta64[ns]

                '3 days 06:00:00', '4 days 04:00:00'],
               dtype='timedelta64[ns]', freq=None)

1

td1 + pd.Timestamp('20200101')

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-02', '2020-01-03', '2020-01-04', '2020-01-05',

IV. Date Offsets

1. Offset Objects

A date offset is a special time delta tied to the calendar. Recall the two questions from Section I: how to find the date of the first Monday in September 2020, and how to find the 30th working day after September 7, 2020.

1

pd.Timestamp('20200831') + pd.offsets.WeekOfMonth(week=0,weekday=0)

Timestamp('2020-09-07 00:00:00')

1

pd.Timestamp('20200907') + pd.offsets.BDay(30)

Timestamp('2020-10-19 00:00:00')

As the examples show, Offset objects are defined in pd.offsets. Using + yields the nearest following date, whereas - yields the nearest preceding date:

1

pd.Timestamp('20200831') - pd.offsets.WeekOfMonth(week=0,weekday=0)

Timestamp('2020-08-03 00:00:00')

1

pd.Timestamp('20200907') - pd.offsets.BDay(30)

Timestamp('2020-07-27 00:00:00')

1

pd.Timestamp('20200907') + pd.offsets.MonthEnd()

Timestamp('2020-09-30 00:00:00')

For the common date offsets, consult the documentation here. Among the Offset objects listed there, one special case deserves mention: CDay, whose holidays, weekmask parameters filter custom dates and weekdays respectively. The former takes a list of dates to exclude; the latter takes a weekday string made of three-letter abbreviations and keeps only the weekdays appearing in that string:

1
2
3

my_filter = pd.offsets.CDay(n=1,weekmask='Wed Fri',holidays=['20200109'])
dr = pd.date_range('20200108', '20200111')
dr.to_series().dt.dayofweek

2020-01-08    2
2020-01-09    3
2020-01-10    4
2020-01-11    5
Freq: D, dtype: int64

1

[i + my_filter for i in dr]

[Timestamp('2020-01-10 00:00:00'),
 Timestamp('2020-01-10 00:00:00'),
 Timestamp('2020-01-15 00:00:00'),
 Timestamp('2020-01-15 00:00:00')]

In the example above, n means adding one CDay. The first date in dr is 20200108, but since the next day 20200109 is excluded and 20200110 is a valid Friday, it becomes 20200110; the later dates are handled similarly.

[CAUTION] Do Not Use Partial Offsets

Due to several bugs in the current version, do not use Offset objects below the Day level, such as Hour, Second; use the corresponding Timedelta objects instead.

[END]

2. Offset Strings

Earlier we noted that the freq of date_range may take an Offset object, and in pandas nearly every Offset object is bound to an offset string (frequencies strings/offset aliases) that can be used in its place. Here are some common examples.

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200331', freq='MS') # # Month start

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-02-01', '2020-03-01'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='MS')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200331', freq='M') # # Month end

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-31', '2020-02-29', '2020-03-31'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='M')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200110', freq='B') # # Business days

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-01-02', '2020-01-03', '2020-01-06',
               '2020-01-07', '2020-01-08', '2020-01-09', '2020-01-10'],
              dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='B')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200201', freq='W-MON') # # Mondays

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-06', '2020-01-13', '2020-01-20', '2020-01-27'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='W-MON')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200201', freq='WOM-1MON') # # First Monday of each month

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-06'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='WOM-1MON')

The strings above are equivalent to using the following Offset objects:

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200331', freq=pd.offsets.MonthBegin())

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-02-01', '2020-03-01'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='MS')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200331', freq=pd.offsets.MonthEnd())

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-31', '2020-02-29', '2020-03-31'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='M')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200110', freq=pd.offsets.BDay())

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-01', '2020-01-02', '2020-01-03', '2020-01-06',
               '2020-01-07', '2020-01-08', '2020-01-09', '2020-01-10'],
              dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='B')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200201', freq=pd.offsets.CDay(weekmask='Mon'))

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-06', '2020-01-13', '2020-01-20', '2020-01-27'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='C')

1

pd.date_range('20200101','20200201', freq=pd.offsets.WeekOfMonth(week=0,weekday=0))

DatetimeIndex(['2020-01-06'], dtype='datetime64[ns]', freq='WOM-1MON')

[CAUTION] A Note on Time Zones

For the development of the various time objects, besides Python’s built-in datetime module, pandas also uses dateutil, largely to handle time zones. As is well known, China has no daylight saving time, but some countries do, making a day relatively 23/24/25 hours long — the relativedelta — so Offset and Timedelta objects may yield different results for the same problem. The rules are fairly complex, parts of the official documentation are inaccurate, and a unified correction is hard because many Offset components are involved. This tutorial therefore disregards time-zone handling entirely; if you are interested in discussing time-zone offsets, you can contact me or see the discussion here.

[END]

V. Rolling Windows and Grouping in Time Series

1. Rolling Windows

A rolling window for time series replaces the window size with the freq keyword. Here is a concrete example: the stock market has an indicator called BOLL, composed of three lines — the middle band, upper band, and lower band — computed respectively as the N-day mean line, the N-day mean plus two N-day standard deviations, and the N-day mean minus two N-day standard deviations. Using a rolling object, the BOLL indicator for N=30 can be written as follows:

1
2
3
4
5
6

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
idx = pd.date_range('20200101', '20201231', freq='B')
np.random.seed(2020)
data = np.random.randint(-1,2,len(idx)).cumsum() # # Simulate a series with a random walk
s = pd.Series(data,index=idx)
s.head()

2020-01-01   -1
2020-01-02   -2
2020-01-03   -1
2020-01-06   -1
2020-01-07   -2
Freq: B, dtype: int32

1
2
3
4
5
6

r = s.rolling('30D')
plt.plot(s)
plt.title('BOLL LINES')
plt.plot(r.mean())
plt.plot(r.mean()+r.std()*2)
plt.plot(r.mean()-r.std()*2)

[<matplotlib.lines.Line2D at 0x26941924dc8>]

For the shift function, when applied to a series indexed by datetime64, freq can be specified to control the shift:

1

s.shift(freq='50D').head()

2020-02-20   -1
2020-02-21   -2
2020-02-22   -1
2020-02-25   -1
2020-02-26   -2
dtype: int32

In addition, applying diff to a datetime64[ns] series yields a timedelta64[ns] series, which lets users conveniently examine the intervals of an ordered time series:

1
2

my_series = pd.Series(s.index)
my_series.head()

0   2020-01-01
1   2020-01-02
2   2020-01-03
3   2020-01-06
4   2020-01-07
dtype: datetime64[ns]

1

my_series.diff(1).head()

0      NaT
1   1 days
2   1 days
3   3 days
4   1 days
dtype: timedelta64[ns]

2. Resampling

The resampling object resample is similar in use to the grouping object groupby in Chapter 4; the former is a grouping object designed for grouped computation on time series.

For example, compute the mean every 10 days for the series above:

1

s.resample('10D').mean().head()

2020-01-01   -2.000000
2020-01-11   -3.166667
2020-01-21   -3.625000
2020-01-31   -4.000000
2020-02-10   -0.375000
Freq: 10D, dtype: float64

If no built-in handler is available, you can define one with apply:

1

s.resample('10D').apply(lambda x:x.max()-x.min()).head() # # Range

2020-01-01    3
2020-01-11    4
2020-01-21    4
2020-01-31    2
2020-02-10    4
Freq: 10D, dtype: int32

In resample, special care is needed with group boundary values. By default, the starting value is computed by adding freq from midnight 00:00:00 of the date corresponding to the minimum timestamp up to the largest timestamp not exceeding that minimum; this timestamp then becomes the start. Each freq is accumulated as a split point for grouping, with intervals left-closed and right-open. Let’s construct an uneven example:

1
2
3
4

idx = pd.date_range('20200101 8:26:35', '20200101 9:31:58', freq='77s')
data = np.random.randint(-1,2,len(idx)).cumsum()
s = pd.Series(data,index=idx)
s.head()

2020-01-01 08:26:35   -1
2020-01-01 08:27:52   -1
2020-01-01 08:29:09   -2
2020-01-01 08:30:26   -3
2020-01-01 08:31:43   -4
Freq: 77S, dtype: int32

The first group below starts at 08:24:00, obtained by adding 72 increments of freq=7 min to midnight; adding another freq would exceed the series’ minimum timestamp 08:26:35:

1

s.resample('7min').mean().head()

2020-01-01 08:24:00   -1.750000
2020-01-01 08:31:00   -2.600000
2020-01-01 08:38:00   -2.166667
2020-01-01 08:45:00    0.200000
2020-01-01 08:52:00    2.833333
Freq: 7T, dtype: float64

Sometimes users want groups formed by starting at the series’ minimum timestamp and incrementing freq; in that case set the origin parameter to start:

1

s.resample('7min', origin='start').mean().head()

2020-01-01 08:26:35   -2.333333
2020-01-01 08:33:35   -2.400000
2020-01-01 08:40:35   -1.333333
2020-01-01 08:47:35    1.200000
2020-01-01 08:54:35    3.166667
Freq: 7T, dtype: float64

Note that the returned index is usually the first timestamp of a group, but for the seven values M, A, Q, BM, BA, BQ, W it is the last timestamp of the corresponding interval:

1
2

s = pd.Series(np.random.randint(2,size=366), index=pd.date_range('2020-01-01', '2020-12-31'))
s.resample('M').mean().head()

2020-01-31    0.451613
2020-02-29    0.448276
2020-03-31    0.516129
2020-04-30    0.566667
2020-05-31    0.451613
Freq: M, dtype: float64

1

s.resample('MS').mean().head() # # Same values, different index

2020-01-01    0.451613
2020-02-01    0.448276
2020-03-01    0.516129
2020-04-01    0.566667
2020-05-01    0.451613
Freq: MS, dtype: float64

df = pd.read_csv('../data/solar.csv', usecols=['Data','Time','Radiation','Temperature'])
